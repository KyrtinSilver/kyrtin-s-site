One of the common sources of public outrage in the modern age has become infidelity scandals, from politicians to tech CEOs (some now “former”). What these events tell us, both about the outraged public and the people in question, are both topics of some interest.

The general public has a habit of “placing public figures on a pedestal”, elevating them above the mundane world via stories of one ideological hero-narrative or another. Many of those figures cultivate these “cults of charisma”, embracing the illusion of being “elite”, or for that matter the illusion of being worthy of any praise at all, as they often aren’t.

A direct consequence of this psychological tendency of the public is that if a public figure embraces that pedestal and the narrative it dictates, and they later betray said narrative, then the public registers the betrayal of the narrative as a personal betrayal, invoking a proportionately strong emotional response. Since this revolves around the narrative itself and how it might be betrayed, the results for two people committing identical actions but embracing different narrative fantasies may vary wildly.

This popular public pass-time of constructing parasocial pedestals is similar to how fans of a sports team may take personal offense when that team’s narrative is attacked or undermined, even if the action comes from a member of the team itself, as they feel it to be a personal betrayal. For example, rationally, Jensen Huang committing 3+ affairs isn’t itself equivalent to a trillion dollar loss in market cap, but he went against the pedestal that he stood upon, and that pedestal may well be equivalent.

Beyond the public and the pedestals that they attempt to place people upon for purposes of “hero worship” and other narrative fantasies, such actions provide much deeper insight into the person in question, which is also why they can so easily cause their own narratives to implode once revealed. One of those insights is the fundamental (or “baseline”) loyalty, or disloyalty, of an individual, upon which all trust in them is built (and/or crumbles).

A romantic relationship is the highest level of intimacy, emotional investment, vulnerability, and trust, that humans are ever likely to form in their lives, and not all humans do. That closeness effectively minimizes barriers, placing the relationship that much closer to the root of loyalty for both parties, leaving less uncertainty for the imagination to fill in. While many still cultivate fantasies even then, they do have a front-row seat to events.

The case of infidelity is nothing new to the tech industry, with a fairly high volume to CEOs counted among publicly known offenders, which led to a few famous divorces and exceedingly wealthy widows. What these events point to is that said individuals are entirely untrustworthy to those deepest relationships that they chose to form, which also means that they are fundamentally untrustworthy in all of their more shallow relationships, all else being equal.

I do say “all else being equal”, since this is a combinatorial problem, as other factors may modify a given outcome or environment, though the above remains the rule, and potent modifiers the exception to it. For example, a sociopathic parasite who studies psychology, lures in a deeply nerdy and highly autistic programmer, then traps him in a marriage of suffering for 20+ years, does constitute a level of combinatorial factors that mark the exception to the rule.

Cause and effect is another noteworthy factor, as it matters immensely which party betrays the other first, if both parties end up betraying one another, as well as the respective degrees of such betrayal. The party who reacts to disloyalty demonstrates retribution (proportionate or otherwise), rather than reflecting their own baseline loyalty.

If a party’s reaction is “forgiveness”, and continued loyalty to the offending party, then they are mentally ill, often proportionate to the offense, and should likely have any decision-making responsibilities over others revoked until any appropriate treatment for that illness has concluded. The crutch of “forgiveness” tends to boil down to an emotional decision where the individual in question chooses to stick to their narrative fantasy, doubling-down on that fantasy via the “Backfire Effect” (a Cognitive Bias) when confronted with evidence that contradicts their chosen narrative.

All of this offers several lessons worth both teaching and learning, applicable to different people in various ways. To public figures, it illustrates that indulging the fantasies of public perception come with risks specific to the fantasies in question, making it paramount that any such indulgence either be chosen wisely, or avoided entirely. To the public, it illustrates that those who are fundamentally disloyal also hold no real loyalty to the pedestals that they are placed upon, even when they take part in shaping the narrative fantasies in question.

Financial markets are also based HEAVILY upon perception and subsequent speculation, primarily and specifically the perceptions and speculation of the small subset of the population who engage with them. This means that any significant impacts to public perception also tend to impact the subset via social influences, leading markets to strongly react to events that may hold little or no strictly rational influence.

Even high-speed trading algorithms tend be operate based upon predicting shifts in those perceptions and speculations, not upon any logical basis. Logical factors creep in by-proxy as they influence shifts in perceptions and speculations, but the algorithms need only predict the outcomes with a slightly better-than-human average result (which can amount to billions of dollars in difference), not each combinatorial factor.

Just as putting a CEO on a pedestal can raise a company’s market cap, or placing a politician on one can raise a political party’s base of support, the destruction of that pedestal can just as easily reverse the process. Since Trust holds a strong asymmetry, being far easier to build than it is to rebuild, the damage of such a pedestal’s destruction can also easily exceed the gains that were extracted while it stood.

Personally, I’ve stated before that my role in events is that of a “doorstop”, I hold the door open that a particular choice may be made, or not, and that is the limit of it. I do this in part to bar the gates against such pedestals and narrative fantasies, stating my purpose, the limits, and drawing a hard line to separate myself from the fantasies of others. Time will tell the value of this approach.
