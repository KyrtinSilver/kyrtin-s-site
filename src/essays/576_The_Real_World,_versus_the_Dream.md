The globally popularized narrative of “The American Dream” has largely been recognized as pure fantasy within the US (even if it was less untrue for specific groups in the past), but it also has some rather interesting consequences that only gain visibility when traveling the world and actively comparing how people think and live around it.

\*Note, this is an abnormally long post.

This subject heavily orients around personal narratives and related Cognitive Biases in practice. When an entire population is indoctrinated to believe things that the statistics don’t align with and that society’s infrastructure makes no real attempt to support, you get something like what we see in the US today.

The “American Dream” tells the narrative story that “If you work hard, then merit dictates that you’ll be rewarded with the comforts of life, a home of your own, the food of your choice, a place for your family, and the financial security for your old age.” Of course, statistics, policies, and actions tell an entirely different story.

Even back in 2010 the Walton family, best known for Walmart, were as wealthy as the lower 41.5% of all other US families combined ([https://www.epi.org/blog/inequality-exhibit-wal-mart-wealth-american/](https://www.epi.org/blog/inequality-exhibit-wal-mart-wealth-american/)). They are also well below the wealth that many others have accumulated today, with noteworthy individuals on that wealth scale appearing dominantly in the tech sector during the 16 years since that time.

13 million US families (roughly 10% of the population) in 2010 even had a “negative net worth”, meaning that they were effectively modern slaves. The economic and social changes that have taken place since that time, such as the rise of “Payday Loans”, absurd rises in costs of living, and systematic legalization and optimization of all forms of addiction, have managed to offset any positive changes in society, keeping this percentage of slaves roughly stable within the US through at least 2019 ([https://finance.yahoo.com/news/13-million-american-households-negative-160705078.html](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/13-million-american-households-negative-160705078.html)).

The term “Meritocracy” has been abused so thoroughly and so many times in the modern age that it often now elicits a knee-jerk reaction at the very mention of it. The concept of merit is not only sound, but supported in principle within nearly every philosophy that one may find, yet it has also never been terribly practical. For thousands of years it has remained appealing, but also utterly unattainable.

When populations such as that of the US are fed the dream of a society where the merit of hard work yields such rewards, but only a small percentage of those who work hard actually reap said rewards (and many of those who reap them didn’t actually earn them), this cultivates mass-resentment, self-loathing, and myriad coping mechanisms that attempt to handle the load of such cognitive dissonance.

This is a part of why that lower 41.5% of US families on the wealth scale are likely to openly resent their jobs and how society treats them, as well as diving into more and more potent coping mechanisms by which they may distract themselves from the dissonance that they resent.

In my travels around the world I’ve met many people who fall well within the lower 20% of the wealth distribution within their respective countries, but across most of these cases I virtually never see such resentment, even though it is usually visible from 50 meters away that I originate from a measurably wealthier country than the one that I’m currently in.

It isn’t hard to imagine people in the US showing significant resentment when faced with someone measurably wealthier than themselves. US citizens generally divide this up across racial lines rather than countries of origin, due in part to the various delusions attached to the US mythos, such as pretending to be “#1”, despite every statistic to the contrary.

As I’ve noted previously, the heavy abuse of various coping mechanisms is also largely absent in many other countries. This difference would seem to be roughly proportionate to the level of cognitive dissonance between social narratives, like myths of meritocracy, and the reality that people actually live in. It is far easier for people to live happily, even within the lower 20% of the wealth distribution, when society doesn’t cultivate such false narratives.

Each culture does have its own kinds of optimization by which it may be distinguished, but false narratives, of meritocracy or otherwise, are more cancers than optimizers. An optimization serves some purpose for a society, offering an equitable trade-off that gains extra value from being better acclimated to some context. False narratives are one of many mechanisms why which such processes may be corrupted and their benefits diverted toward one kind of parasite or another.

Rather than experiencing the typical default response within the US, resentment and division, a genuine appreciation is the more common occurrence elsewhere. This translates to a higher default level of respect and greater appreciation for every action above the default expectations, even across hard language barriers.

For example, every Lunar New Year I give the staff at my location the traditional “Lucky Money”, an envelope especially for the occasion that some amount of cash is placed within. I place the equivalent of $4 into each, but that represents a far greater amount than most of those I hand it to will receive from anyone else, many of whom may only make the equivalent of under $300 per month in salary. Importantly, this is appreciated, rather than expected, and appreciated all the more for being unexpected.

Similar traditions may be found in many Chinese-influenced and/or adjacent cultures, where luck is recognized as a strong factor rather than attributing everything to delusions of meritocracy. Even in China itself, famous for relentless social forces aimed at hard work and merit, that old anchor of Luck factoring heavily into tradition serves to mitigate some of the harms seen in places that focus more exclusively on delusions of merit, such as the “American Dream”.

Another social factor of interest when considering societies globally is that many societies are continuing to follow an overall upward social trajectory, even as the US decays and collapses under the weight of increasing absurdities. Some factors still lag behind, operating on longer time-scales, such as the build-out of infrastructure within major cities that have seen the most growth in recent decades.

This time-lag leads to a patchwork of areas that are constantly having fresh work done to improve basic infrastructure, and in some places you might see a 4,000-apartment mega-structure across the street from a tin shack, but many such places continue to move in the right direction. The newer infrastructure build-outs also benefit from the fruits of global experience and technological progress, with the train system in Bangkok far superior to that of London or the Netherlands, while also costing 5 to 10 times less.

People who aren’t busy diving deep into every potent coping mechanism that the world may offer also tend to manage their finances far more responsibly. I’ve known several people making upwards of $250,000 per year in tech who live far worse lives than I have on $25,000 per year, during these past 4 years of traveling the world. I’m also significantly healthier for it than they are today.

There are still some places that more closely mirror the various toxic cultures found in the US, such as the “Hustle Culture” of manic slave labor found in tourist traps like Bali, where one can measure the distance that they’ve walked by how many times they’ve been offered a massage or a ride. However, tourist traps are the exception, not the norm, and likely a strong contributor to various delusions that many in the US and similar countries hold about how the rest of the world lives. Bali, for example, serves as Australia’s equivalent of Cancun, Mexico, a tourist trap where drunken suckers flock in droves, with the local ecology adapted to exploit them.

Even in such traps, it is far more often the tourists getting drunk, doing drugs, and smoking than the locals. The locals may adapt to extract the funds of cancerous influences, but they rarely adopt their ways in a more complete sense.

The exception to this tends to be found in the upper segment of wealth within many societies. I can find multi-story shopping malls containing numerous familiar stores within virtually any country that I visit, many of which safely out-do those typical of the US. You’ll see far more tourists at these locations than elsewhere, but you also still mostly see the fraction of locals who can afford the prices of international brands and goods.

In other words, all of those expensive goods still exist, and they still serve visible roles in the economy, but they don’t operate on such a merit-heavy narrative. People are still perfectly happy without them, but may buy them if they’re able.

The US is a place known for its “car culture”, to the point that most places in the US aren’t even feasible to live in without one, even while the US paradoxically seems to have far worse drivers, on average, which further contributes to the extortion of “car insurance”.

In contrast, The Netherlands has a strong biking culture including many without motors, and extensive bike paths by which cities and towns are commonly navigated for everyday purposes. Much of Southeast Asia has a strong motorized bike culture, and indeed most deliveries and transportation operate on this dynamic, with people commonly hopping on the back of a “Grab” bike for transportation to work, or having one of those same drivers deliver food.

I’ve said before that Hollywood movies could hire stunt drivers from the streets of Southeast Asian cities in particular with relative ease, as the level of skill required to drive is far greater, and a big part of the difference between how people drive in the US and there is a matter of social dynamics.

What you see on the streets of such cities covered in motorbikes is a virtual absence of many common rules in the US or UK. Bikes are expected to sometimes move onto and even park on what might otherwise be called sidewalks, but they operate in tandem with foot traffic, cooperating more like a collective intelligence or hive than the “competitive driving” mentality found in hyper-individualistic cultures like the US.

The average “competitive driver” is absolute crap in practical terms, which leads to more frequent traffic accidents. The lethality of accidents is still higher on motorbikes, of course, but the rate of lethal traffic accidents is still fairly close between the US and much of Southeast Asia. By the numbers, 11.3-17.7 deaths per 100,000 in 3 countries I’ve visited in Southeast Asia (Indonesia, Malaysia, and Vietnam), as opposed to the US’s 14.2 traffic deaths per 100,000 ([https://worldpopulationreview.com/country-rankings/road-deaths-by-country](https://worldpopulationreview.com/country-rankings/road-deaths-by-country)). The only meaningfully more dangerous country is Thailand, sitting at 25.4. The real winners by this metric are mostly European countries, from 1.5 in Norway to 3.4 in the Netherlands, where smaller but more protective vehicles (than motorbikes) are common.

By another measure, the US is ranked 8th globally, with 779 registered motor vehicles per 1,000 people, as of 2023, while Vietnam is ranked 147th, with only 43 per 1,000, as of 2020 ([https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_and_territories_by_motor_vehicles_per_capita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_and_territories_by_motor_vehicles_per_capita))

The net result is that even when crossing the street in Southeast Asia can look to a US citizen like absolute chaos, doing so tends to be safer than crossing the street in the US. There is a learning curve, as you have to move as the swarm moves, not as an individual following some default set of rules, but the benefits are worth the effort.

The growing disparity between “dreams” like the American one, and the realities of the world also come into focus through another lens from one of the dependencies of such dreams, housing.

This takes several very different forms, where “Luxury” (grossly overpriced) housing was frequently being built and left 80%+ empty for the first year or two in places like Seattle. In contrast, countries like the UK and Netherlands have both suffered severe housing shortages that have persisted for a decade or more, where the necessary additional houses simply aren’t being built. Neither can claim to satisfy such dreams of a good life when housing is either grossly overpriced or not built at all. For example, in the Netherlands many rentals are actually taken within minutes of being posted, and services explicitly designed to let you know about them a few minutes early are popular for this reason.

In contrast, the cities whose populations have grown the most in recent decades across Southeast Asia have seen the rise of countless mega-structures for housing purposes. The details of structures built to imitate their counterparts in other countries can feel a little unpolished, where the technique of their installation might fall a little short, but the structures supply the necessary housing without the dominantly empty “luxury” buildings or lack of housing noted above. Even with much of the infrastructure still playing catch-up in these locations, they’ve made it work.

Part of these differences are cultural, but much of it can be traced back to the stories that people tell themselves, which span a wider spectrum than culture, and such differences frequently offer their own strong advantages. Most cultures don’t harbor strong isolationist (or “nationalist”) streaks, with notable exceptions being the US and Japan, and wherever those streaks remain absent then it remains far easier to enjoy the benefits of such cultural advantages.

There is only one real thing that the US has demonstrably remained first in, by all that I’ve seen and every statistic inspected, which is that it is the only steaming pile of sh\*t that doesn’t have much valid reason to be the way that it is. One of the countries that I spent time in during my travels was the only country remaining today whose capital is still split in two by an occupying (and larger) hostile neighbor, Cyprus.

The country has a long history of being invaded again and again, trading ruling hands across the centuries, with it spending the last 60 years split into 4 different pieces by the last conflict, Cyprus proper, Turkish occupied Cyprus, a Demilitarized Zone between them, and several foreign military bases. The psychological and economic scars of this reality were plain to see as I stayed in the Cypriot portion of the country.

The net result of all of those horrible things happening to Cyprus was no better or worse than all of the same status quo that I saw for 30 years across the US, the difference being that the US has no such excuse for being a complete Sh\*thole. If anything, Cyprus was still slightly better overall, and the locals who I met there were far better than their average US counterparts.

This is the irony of the “American Dream”, the rest of the world tends to satisfy the criteria of that dream far better than the US does, while still being far more happy on average even below that bar. Some elsewhere in the world still naively believe the sales pitch of that dream, but those both competent and financially able tend to leave the US behind permanently with growing frequency, so even the PR of that “dream” may fade faster as people outside of the US meet those who left it behind.
