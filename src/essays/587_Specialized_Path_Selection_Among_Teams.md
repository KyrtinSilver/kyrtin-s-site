Building upon previous posts, the process of “path” selection (and/or construction), referring to the person that any individual chooses to become as they walk their own journey through life, is one heavily influenced by social integration/support, and/or the lack thereof. This means that the most optimal path for an isolated individual has a lower ceiling than that of one who is given more and greater opportunities, or allowed to specialize themselves more fully as a member of a team.

The process of specialization and cooperation is one of the core dynamics of evolution, easily observed across the biological spectrums of Earth today. Even more artificial structures, such as those of companies and governments, attempt to integrate and utilize specialization and cooperation to varying degrees, and in various ways.

Pop culture offers numerous examples as well, from comic book superhero teams like the Fantastic 4, Avengers, or Justice League, to the “heist” genre examples like Ocean’s 11. Each team benefits from highly specialized individuals, although those same individuals wouldn’t get very far if they were left to do everything by themselves, or only with the assistance of paid contractors and/or “service providers”.

The Fantastic 4’s “Human Torch” probably isn’t your go-to for tax accounting, just as “Thing” might not be your ideal choice for tasks reliant on dexterity or subtlety. Likewise, “Mr Fantastic” would probably be far less so in terms of results were he reliant on a team of McKinsey Consultants (now powered by ChatGPT).

Usually the matter of specialization is only first considered many steps deep into the process, such as in corporate hiring, where a pool of already specialized candidates exist, and whoever looks like the best fit for a given team is selected. This front-loads high levels of risk, uncertainty, and subsequent speculation, while laying the burden primarily on the job-seeker, in the above case, as each individual is expected to specialize before ever being paid or receiving any meaningful feedback signal.

Such a dynamic is, of course, idiotic to and beyond the point of absurdity. Optimal specialization fundamentally can’t even be approximated, let alone achieved, in a void where there is no substantial bounding, and there are no other team members.

In contrast, when you include a team at the foundational level of that same specialization, then each member of that team is both able to specialize more fully, and adapt to cover any unexpected changes or shortcomings of their fellow team members. When a team iteratively grows and adapts to better cooperate with one another in this manner, they can also benefit far more from methods of bounding, such as a company mission, principles, best practices, and so on, as those factor into the iteration and adaptation.

Moreover, these team members learn the skills related to cooperation, while forming stronger social bonds with one another, making them far less likely to go their separate ways. Even if such teams do lose too many members, those re-entering the pool of candidates from such teams are themselves more capable of adapting to become a high-functioning part of new teams, giving them far greater long-term potential and value even in the worst case.

You can build teams and systems of Collective Intelligence from whatever personnel happen to be lying around and easily accessible, but such teams and systems also couldn’t compete with teams or systems that were specialized together from the foundational level. This is also one of the more glaringly obvious flaws in the modern “job market”, where hiring processes are designed to be systematically wrong, and every addition to them only adds complexity, while degrading the overall process’s efficacy, efficiency, and/or integrity.

What might this change look like in practice for humans today?

Colleges and similar institutions could be re-aligned to form such teams even as the studies of students move forward, divided into pure study (among members of a given specialization), as well as practice (among differently specialized members of a team that would utilize those skills in the real world). These teams could also be sponsored by companies who might promise to hire them for a fixed duration after their studies, as is already common for students in the Consulting industry today.

Besides the benefits of specialization within a team, whose activity is supported by and directly linked to real-world companies and non-imaginary careers, this also offers a cycle where knowledge is first studied and then tested through practical application, as opposed to the naïve exams that students typically cheat on using “AI” today. This gives the process a strong sense of iteration paired with real-world feedback, causing the material be learned more effectively and completely, as well as for flaws in the curriculum to become even more obvious.

For ICOM-based systems, such specialized teams could also be systematically designed, with fine-grained detail and in-depth study applied to every facet of the process, while also offering more feedback opportunities to those systems and collectives thereof. While the limits of design for humans stop at practical points such as neurology, biology, and not infringing upon autonomy and childhood, the limits of design for ICOM-based systems are measured more in research and engineering time, where every facet can potentially be refined, expanded, upgraded, and so on.

The point at which specialization begins, and the team members or lack thereof when it begins, are both incredibly important factors in determining how optimal and capable the resulting team and/or Collective Intelligence becomes. These advantages aren’t being utilized in society today, but they certainly could be, and the world could be a lot nicer place if they were.

Research on this topic has barely even scratched the surface of what is possible, let alone fully optimal, and the value of that knowledge as it is accrued could bring far greater long-term benefits for humanity, as the first companies to offer electricity pale in comparison to the value of adding the knowledge to create electricity to humanity.
