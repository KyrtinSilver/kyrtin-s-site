One of the highest impact Cognitive Biases in real-world application comes from people estimating the costs and benefits of an opportunity, while also failing to recognize the complete costs of missing that opportunity. In practice, the difference between the two is often the naïve assumption of a neutral outcome versus a negative outcome (true cost).

Through the lens of Cognitive Bias, and Cognition more broadly, this makes perfect sense, as computing the true negative impact of a missed opportunity adds further layers of complexity. It is far easier, no matter how wildly inaccurate, to assume that no change occurs if the opportunity is missed, as fewer factors need be considered, and those that are often require far more shallow evaluations.

A famous example in the business world, often cited in the research literature, comes from the downfall of Nokia, where they were a leading force in their industry for a time, but by missing the opportunity of “smart phones” they quickly fell into obscurity. No doubt they recognized the cost and potential benefits of the opportunity, but they also failed to consider the true cost of missing that same opportunity.

I’ve written about this in posts and a research paper before as well, with more extreme examples noted in the “Ethical Basilisk Thought Experiment”, where additional factors like Ethical Pillars and Centers of Gravity come into play to extend the dynamics. This is also why I keep a “grey-list” of companies, investors, and other organizations who’ve taken deeply unethical actions, with that grey-list due to turn completely black at a specific point, beyond which the weight of their crimes against humanity no longer have any viable mechanisms by which they may recover and be repaid.

Beyond the costs and benefits of opportunities, as well as the true costs of missed opportunities, there are also the consequences of the alternatives to those opportunities that were instead selected. The current example of “AI-induced Psychosis” is one of the more obvious instances of such a consequence, where degenerative alternatives to actual opportunities were selected, and the consequences add their own kinds of damage on top of the true costs of missing the core opportunity itself.

If the true costs of a missed opportunity are virtually never computed in practice, then the combination of those true costs and the consequential costs of alternatives to those opportunities instead being selected must themselves be rarer still. Related cognitive bias research comes from Kahneman and Tversky on the Conjunction Fallacy and “Linda the bank teller”, since it is inherently less probable to have the conjunction of more specific conditions than fewer or more general ones. See also: [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjunction_fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjunction_fallacy)

Some of the most severe problems in the world today are themselves consequences of the alternative paths to opportunities, or “altpportunities”, being selected, including the creation of the current AI Bubble, and all consequences that follow it popping. Had the actual cutting edge of AI technology been properly funded back in 2022, rather than the dumpster fire of LLMs, then one war could have been cut short (Ukraine), several other wars prevented, and countless less severe and/or less obvious problems avoided entirely.

Even now, the consequences of such poor decision-making at global scales continue to steadily, and often predictably, grow in the direction of human extinction. No one extinction risk dominates in isolation, but rather they each tend to be co-morbid to the rest, with increasing risks of one kind also escalating others in a cascade of consequences that plays out across 2nd and 3rd order effects.

However much effect “FOMO” (The Fear of Missing Out) may hold over gullible populations, such impulses are no substitute for actual cognition covering the same costs, as opposed to the fear of risks. Instead, one who applies cognition and perceives more of costs and consequences might have “ROMO” (The Reality of Missing Out).
