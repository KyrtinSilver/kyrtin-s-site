One of the stranger quirks of human cognition and cognitive biases comes in the form of a passive tendency to assign credit for events and developments while entirely neglecting all other past alternatives that could have just as easily produced the same, or even vastly superior, results. In line with the naming conventions of similar cognitive biases, I’ll refer to this phenomena as “Illusory Inevitability”.

This cognitive bias effectively assumes optimality of choice when individuals apply it to the task of assigning credit, something that is often wildly unlikely in any statistical sense. For example, if 10 people have the potential to create the same thing, each able to do so at varying speeds and to some variable of resulting quality, then this cognitive bias assumes that whoever receives the opportunity to do so must have also been the optimal choice, delivering both the fastest and highest quality results.

The divide between this narrative fantasy propped up by such cognitive biases, and the reality from which it is statistically so thoroughly divorced only grows more extreme as more uncertainty is added to the mix. There are a slew of cognitive biases that are distinctly pro-fantasy, tied deeply to both emotional perceptions and memory, making it none too surprising how “fate” and other terms for inevitability are so very popular in fantasy literature, even as companies attempt to imply or invoke the same within their own PR narratives.

The past is simply the past, it wasn’t inevitable that events must occur in a certain way, even if many counterfactual possibilities do converge on the same outcomes later on.

Wars can be avoided. Ethical people can be elected. Dystopian tech CEOs can be shot in the head. All are possible, and none are inevitable.

A critical part of decision-making for planning any path forward is recognizing not only what came before, but the flaws in the path that was taken, and what decisions could have been made to prevent them. If a subset of those flaws are merely treated as though the means (or ends) were inevitable, then that subset remains a blind spot in the decision-making process moving forward, and many such blind spots are discovered and exploited by bad actors, sooner or later.

Statistics frequently tell the story of outright failures being far more common than success in the business world, with ~90% of startups failing, and another ~75% of the subset of startups backed by major investors still failing. Even if one were to generously assume the other 25% to have been wise investments, one can just as safely say that the failed 75% were not, or at least that most were far from being optimal choices.

“Red Ocean Markets” in particular, where many businesses attempt to do the same thing, sell the same products and services, etc., frequently demonstrate such abysmal statistics. In contrast, most investors systematically avoid “Blue Ocean Markets”, places where new value is created and offered, making their own high failure rates that much more predictable.

Other statistics focused on financial markets are equally telling, like between 63-97% of traders losing money, variation depending on the type of trading activity, with most quitting the job in the first 2 years. Their choices were obviously not even close to being optimal, and they were just as far from being inevitable.

One of the benefits of ICOM-based (and likely ICOM-inspired) technology is that memory-related cognitive biases such as this have no draw, no reason to use them. A system that can simply remember events as they occurred, without degradation over time, doesn’t have the same incentive to construct simplified narratives of inevitability.

One of the central pillars of Ethics is autonomy, as many basic rights revolve around that core concept. While different cultures may adopt and emphasize fractions of that constellation of rights in their own distinct ways, the entire set of rights remains fundamental to Ethics.

Even if the universe is purely deterministic, something that could be truly difficult to prove, the basic rights (and subsequent responsibilities) orbiting autonomy don’t change based upon that determinism one way or the other. In reality, I’ve never come across less than 3 core choices, usually with countless variations branching out from them, and none among them are inevitable.

Determinism’s more extreme fanboys are virtually indistinguishable from “hollow Nihilists” (people who continue to live because they do in fact believe in the value of eating and breathing, directly contrary to their stated beliefs), because claiming that every choice is inevitable is rationally equivalent to claiming that no choice matters (via claiming that choice itself is illusory). It can’t matter that a choice was made if there was no choice to begin with after all.

Many elements of human cognition are directly or indirectly reliant on the presence of choice, or the illusion of it as the case may be, which is part of why the more visible instances where “inevitability” is invoked tend to boil down to one coping mechanism or another. The less visible instances where such illusory inevitability is assumed and left to omission can be passive coping mechanisms as well, but they can also be oversights.

In both cases, coping mechanism or oversight, policy-makers across the geopolitical spectrum frequently demonstrate this cognitive bias, and through such demonstrations it is made clear that their own choices fall short of optimality, alongside those where such bias is applied. Sometimes their nations and the world can afford the cost of the difference, and sometimes they can’t.

Better choices could have been made in the past, to reach a more peaceful and prosperous present, just as better choices can still be made moving forward.
