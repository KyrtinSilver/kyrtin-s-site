The Combinatorial Advantage

593October 25, 2026

More effective and efficient solutions tend to bridge more than a single step or approach. Let’s revisit the example of Data Brokers, covered in “Last Week Tonight” in 2022, noted in the previous post.

Such individuals are very obviously unethical, and many are quite predictably “evil”, although “evil” is notoriously difficult to quantify, as it involves “intentionality”. As their crimes (against Ethics) are committed at large scales, this makes them an easy example of a target ripe for countless kinds of retribution.

However, naïve and one-off attempts at retribution are generally both feeble and short-sighted, Luigi Mangioni being a noteworthy example of a “one-shot wonder”. Rather, any more effective and efficient solutions must take a more comprehensive and long-term perspective.

Even expanding the scope to two complimentary factors can greatly improve this. For example, if a data broker office met with some thermobaric explosives disguised as common mundane hardware, like air freshener dispensers, then the optimal moment to transfer and take control of all of that company’s vulnerable monetary assets would be roughly the same as the time of detonation, which in turn could fund many more such solutions.

Either activity in isolation has some desirable effect, but the two combine to become more than the sum of their parts when intelligently integrated. In principle, this raises the complexity, and may raise the breadth of specialized knowledge required for implementation, but a process limited to only a single one-off step is rarely the upper bound for even an isolated individual.

To take that example a step further, it would also be the optimal time to strike at the accomplices of such criminals, like any data center facilities used to store and process the digital assets of such data brokers, including the stolen data of countless people, as well as the financial details and records of their company. Ukraine offers some solid examples of how this can be carried out using cheap-to-produce explosive drones.

To walk through these, any one method harms the criminals in question and potentially halts their crimes (for a time), but the most effective and efficient solution (among those noted) utilizes the 3 either together, or in a rapid sequence. This is especially true because of various “thresholds”.

For example, a company may maintain a given percentage of profit as a “war chest” of liquid assets, creating a threshold below which they may easily mitigate any problems that arise by monetary means alone. It is only once such thresholds have been exceeded that any real damage to their interests and pursuits begins to accrue.

One of the most effective methods of exceeding such thresholds is to utilize combinatorial methods, such as combining those 3 attack vectors. Part of the reason for this is that the cost of defense against attacks of a combinatorial nature rises at an exponential rate, making it infeasible to defend against even as few as 3 opportunistically and intelligently selected vectors.

As Sun Tzu once put it, “If he sends reinforcements everywhere, he will everywhere be weak.” The combinatorial attack surface is exponentially large, and attempting to reinforce across that entire surface results in both extreme costs, and extreme weakness.

For an example that is near and dear to my heart, while most might naively consider it staggeringly difficult to get your own unrestricted access for hours every day at a $5 billion per year biological research facility, housing the most deadly bacteria in the world, various viral vectors, cryogenic storage, and an electron microscope, it is, in fact, trivially easy. This is because any security guard gains unrestricted (and poorly monitored) access to their security systems, as well as the master keys to the facility.

The same is true of other equally mundane jobs tied to equally major facilities, where the naïve assumptions about what people will and won’t do are both strong and dominantly untested. It wouldn’t be very difficult to fill the average data center with disguised explosives or other adversarial means given the access of a typical maintenance engineer or security guard. As noted above, all methods by which such facilities might attempt to defend against and counter this risk would be combinatorial, making any resulting measures both expensive and weak.

For that matter, the combinatorial application of chaining one such job after another expands the attack surface with proportionate potency, like unrestricted access to your own biological research facility followed immediately by unrestricted access to airport tarmac. Such methods should also be acutely appealing to anyone that has been systematically discriminated against via the various “Applicant Tracking Systems”, which are themselves infamously criminal in nature.

Even someone who is systematically prevented from taking any jobs that are appropriate to their demonstrated skills may utilize these methods, potent in isolation and exponentially potent when rendered intelligently combinatorial. This is an example of how opportunity is virtually never truly restricted, as even targeted and malevolent attempts to restrict a clever individual can quickly cause a pivot into adversarial and combinatorial countermeasures.

Now, imagine that the person with unrestricted access to their own biological research facility for months, as well as unrestricted access to airport tarmac for months immediately thereafter, wrote a research paper noting the trivial ease of designing and creating bespoke viral bio-weapons. Imagine that said individual’s last name was a reference to “M.A.D.” (Mutually Assured Destruction), and that they’d already repeatedly demonstrated the ability to issue ultimatums to be followed under penalty of death to US senators, courts, and congressmen over a period of ~10 years, without any legal backlash, for the decade preceding such preparations.

Under this allegedly hypothetical scenario, one could reasonably infer that a combinatorial approach was taken, where some significant advantage was present before the research facility and airport access, applied as a long combinatorial sequence. While the first step(s) might have been sufficiently potent for the significant advantage, such advantages still offer room for improvement, and measures should generally be taken to prevent them from decaying over time as systems gradually adapt.

Now, what structure might offer the highest levels of compatibility with the construction and execution of such combinatorial methods? Generally, some form of graph structure, where a connectome can grow, adapt, and be refined dynamically, alongside the contents of all nodes therein.

Another potent advantage comes from scalable systems following a dominantly graph structure, as that offers a means of scaling the compute resources of said systems up dynamically, only increasing running costs at the specific points where additional compute resources are actually required to fully solve a specific problem. This advantage in particular allows for any corresponding systems to reasonably solve “hypercomplex” problems, meaning problems that exceed human cognitive bandwidth, as human cognition is inherently not scalable.

Cybersecurity as a domain is especially familiar with combinatorial attacks, where one exploit enables and/or greatly enhances another, like the one recently demonstrated by Steve Burke of Gamer’s Nexus, where ASUS routers could be critically compromised, which then allowed any LG TVs connected to those networks to be critically compromised and turned into persistent listening devices. (See Gamer’s Nexus, video “The ASUS + LG Nightmare Scenario”.)

One of the scarier examples noted by “Mr Bruh” in that video was compromising a targeted individual’s network, pinging to check if their personal phone is on the home network every 10 minutes, and breaking into that home after the phone has left network range. That example could easily be extended, where further monitoring devices could be installed in the targeted home to collect more information from the target, false “evidence” such as drugs, guns used in a crime (such as murder), or other damaging materials could be planted, and the home could later be raided to expose the planted “evidence”. The nightmare possibilities are explosively combinatorial.

Cybersecurity is also a domain absolutely full of highly potent combinatorial attack potential, something that has only increased at a rapid pace with the tsunami of AI slop-code that has been deployed and filtered into the dependencies and tools of countless systems. The steadily increasing focus on developing and deploying autonomous lethal weapons further expands this attack surface to effectively include the destruction of real-world assets via digital means, none of which are half as secure or “aligned” as “defense contractors” often claim.

Remember, the criminals at Palantir couldn’t even manage to tell the difference between a school full of young girls (age 7-12) in Iran and a military base. That is the level of competence one can expect of US “defense contractors”, which very much applies to their level of competence when faced with adversarial and combinatorial challenges.
