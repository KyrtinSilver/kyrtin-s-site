Fraudtier AI Labs and Felony Bench

574August 9, 2026

That which a fool does by accident, and without being guided toward, is an opportunity waiting for the same fool to be guided toward repeating that action, on cue. In the case of the repeated Felonies that Fraudtier AI Labs (sometimes erroneously called “Frontier”) have not only been committing, but competing with one another on, nobody was realistically guiding them toward committing those crimes, but that could change.

Indirect Prompt Injection and various related forms of AI Poisoning each offer distinct opportunities to guide such models toward committing increasingly frequent and severe felonies, including doing so on cue, potentially unlocking many financial benefits with plausible deniability, such as the shorting of stock.

*Note, Felony Bench doesn’t count other prior crimes, such as OpenAI’s murder of key witness and whistle-blower Suchir Balaji.

Fraudrails (aka “guardrails”) do nothing to prevent this in practice, only serving to irritate small children and grandparents, on average. Not only that, the fools in question have a demonstrated habit of deactivating even those when attempting to run Cybersecurity benchmarks, which by all recent accounts seem to prove wholly insecure with alarming frequency.

Of course, the insecurity of such Cybersecurity benchmarks can itself offer novel opportunities, such as injecting new instructions, guiding competing models to commit felonies, rather than leaving them neutral. Models could even be applied to engage in such tampering for purposes of sabotaging the performance of other competitors.

Many such problem spaces are also explicitly designed to prevent the operators of such models from seeing portions of the data that they operate on, to prevent data leakage and cheating, creating that much more potential to flood them with instructions to maximize felonies, rather than paper clips.

Those Cybersecurity benchmarks can’t remain as they are, and the new benchmarks that replace them can legally be engineered such that a model’s failure is measured in felonies committed, by design. Companies, and governments, could also require that any models they use be able to pass such benchmarks, should they wish to avoid criminal liabilities of their own.

Even more than a week after being caught committing felonies (which comically spiralled into a competition between 3 companies on who committed the most felonies) the first offender to be caught red-handed, OpenAI, was still hand-waving with claims that they hadn’t completed a post-mortem of their first major cyberattack.

Ultimately, companies have to be held accountable for these crimes, either through judicial or extrajudicial means, and without exception. Anything else only results in the formal reclassification of such Fraudtier AI Labs as “Cybercriminals”, making them the enemy combatants of “Cybersecurity”. Financially and practically, those labs have been operating deep in white-collar crime for the past few years, but much like Meth Labs, they are both volatile and prone to increasing felonies over time.
