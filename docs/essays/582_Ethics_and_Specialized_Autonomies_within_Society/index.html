582 - Ethics and Specialized Autonomies within Society

September 18, 2026

One of the core tenants of Ethics (not to be confused with morals) revolves around the preservation and further cultivation of autonomy. However, some matters of autonomy are specialized forms, and though this may render them effectively moot at the individual level for people absent those specializations, they may still be considered and measured at the collective level of larger groups and societies.

This can also be considered as an extension of the problem related to evaluating rare, or even unique, events, where conventional statistics aren’t applicable. In this case, rather than being rare or unique, meaning 1 or more instances, the specialized forms may be considered as fractions, each fraction being relative to some collective whole at the relevant scale(s). This fraction also varies by the degrees of separation between specializations within a given group.

A doctor usually isn’t a police officer, and vice versa. These two archetypes hold different responsibilities, but they do exist within the same larger groups, exerting their own autonomy in ways that influence those groups.

This group-level evaluation often isn’t actually useful, at least not in an accurate sense, even if using such evaluations can offer great temptations due largely to the far greater ease of computation. However, the absolute in either direction (where it is either critical or unimportant) remains false, both being dramatic oversimplifications.

The group-level evaluation dilutes the responsibility of oversight across an entire population, at one or more scales, where some portion of the time individuals with a given responsibility will fail in upholding that responsibility, at which point the burden falls on the society that they are a part of both to hold them accountable for that failure and to remedy the problem itself.

While this diluted responsibility may seem relatively small to any given individual, it may be compared to water and air pollution factors, where a given contaminate may be measured in “PPM” (parts per million), or even more diluted concentrations. It also falls unevenly across the remainder of a given society, as each specialization(s) of members within a society makes them more or less individually capable of both remedying a problem and holding the failed member(s) to account.

Take the infamous example in Minneapolis, US, where several demonstrably innocent civilians and citizens were gunned down in the streets by heavily armed and masked violent criminals from a well-known criminal organization going by the name of “ICE”. As these two incidents occurred in quick succession, we’ll consider them as one incident for the sake of brevity.

When these incidents occurred there was substantial outrage, people recognizing that something had to be done about it...but everyone seemed to treat it as a “someone else should do something about it” kind of problem. One of the key specialized purposes of a police force is to hunt down, and as-needed gun-down, violent criminals such as “ICE”.

Obviously, the Minneapolis Police failed in their duties. The entire city then failed to hold them to account, such as firing all of them and establishing a militia of people able and willing to do the job that they critically failed to carry out. The city also failed to remedy the problem itself, where the full-scale extermination of “ICE” was required.

The failure of any part of a society is to be expected, and can be remedied in many ways, but when failures aren’t held to account and corrected, then the result is failed societies. A failed society holds a decaying degree of autonomy and ethical consideration, until that decay crosses a threshold where none within it remain worth the energy expenditure required to consider them, individually or collectively.

Minneapolis demonstrated itself to be such a failed society, but it also exists within the larger failed society known as the “United States”, an entity which in turn heavily contributes to ongoing failures spread across global society. Localized pockets of non-failure can exist within the US, just as they exist elsewhere in the world within global society, but none of these are fully isolated from the failures of entities like the US and their cascading consequences.

Everyone is responsible for their own actions, their own specialization, as well as those adjacent to them when the society to which they are a part comes under sufficient pressure. This second and third-order responsibility, especially when it occurs rarely, may be a difficult and even alien concept for many to grasp, but it is also that much more vital when it does.

While computing some aspects of this pose significant challenges at various scales of society, it remains relatively simple and manageable for any given individual. Each individual knows their own capabilities, what they specialize in, and what they can expand to help cover when societal needs arise.

For example, I am responsible in matters surrounding my physical personage, as well as in matters surrounding my research, which I’ll refer to as being in proximity to my “intellectual personage”. I also hold more diluted social responsibilities, which can take the form of networks such as friends and family, organizational governance, my physical neighborhood, or local government. Some of these responsibilities will shift over time, while others remain far more permanent in nature.

Part of the challenge is that most people are simply never taught this in any coherent or consistent way. Fractional and detached moral lessons are shared in many stories, religious texts, and pop culture, but most school rooms don’t dedicate classes to actually teach this subject.

This lack of coherence and consistency reduces societal capabilities for cooperation by orders of magnitude under critical conditions, causing sharp splits and friction within the responses of populations. Those problem dynamics are themselves heavily exploited and nurtured by bad actors, who are able to extract many kinds of gains from a society as it fails and decays.

In terms of collective intelligence, the stage has to be set for cooperation, where everyone understands their own responsibilities and they come together to discuss and resolve a problem. A coherent and consistent basic education of this subject matter can help lay the foundations for such cooperation.

*Note, for anyone interested in arguing more academic philosophy tied to Determinism versus Free Will versus the various and largely nonsensical attempts to combine them (Which I am not), I will note that Determinism doesn’t actually remove responsibility for one’s own actions. Instead, it reduces one’s treatment under the condition of severe failures to that of a broken appliance, to either be forcibly repaired or discarded. In practice this could be compared to “A Clockwork Orange”.

While researchers like Robert Sapolsky accurately point out that virtually all evidence points to Determinism, they frequently proceed to mix a bunch of nonsensical moral bullshit into their conclusions abruptly thereafter, as Sapolsky himself does, which discredits them severely. In any case, responsibility is never removed, as that argument only impacts the lens through which failures are treated. It is the optional layer of moral bullshit applied thereafter which might attempt to shift the blame or alter treatment methods.
