The entire “piracy” issue is better considered as a 2nd or 3rd order effect of extremely poor and entirely irrational choices in both public policy and corporate strategy. Take the example of how video streaming piracy has increased over recent years, where the causes are blatantly obvious:

• In the following years, a bunch of copycat companies, including major movie studios, Amazon, Apple, etc. all attempt to make their own sleazy imitations of Netflix, each wanting to charge their own subscription fee.

• Each of these sleazy imitation companies inflates the operating cost to Netflix while siphoning off some of their more moronic customers.

• The selection of each company, both Netflix and all of the sleazy imitations, turns to absolute shit, making none of them worth paying for.

• ...All of which could have been avoided if the sleazy Netflix imitations had been ground into a fine paste of Soylent Green. Fuck them all.

The same basic problem of sleazy imitation companies crowding a market and turning it into a “Red Ocean” of absolute shit has played out repeatedly across many industries, with similar results. This is partly because the above is an instance of a “Tragedy of the Commons”, where a finite communal resource suffers from an influx of major parasitic infection, with each new parasite attempting to maximize what it can extract.

As a 2nd or 3rd order effect, much like drug abuse, piracy can only be used as a metric for measuring the efficacy of public policy and corporate strategy, within the context of metrics that encompass all other changes that they incur, such as the severe reputational damage of a company choosing to use Denuvo. Attempts to target and prevent it directly are every bit as absurd as the US’s decades long and laughably failed “War on Drugs”.

Considering that “War on Drugs” approach of the US also offers a long-term study of how such a blatantly idiotic policy of “waging war” on a 2nd or 3rd order effect plays out in practice. Fighting the 2nd or 3rd order effect directly only causes it to undergo an evolutionary process as it is refined and proliferates. It also diverts funding away from solutions actually capable of addressing the root causes of these effects, increasing the baseline pressure and frequency of the underlying issues.

Similar dynamics can also be observed in the domains of “insurance” and “marketing” scammers, where their presence and parasitic activities directly harm the subject of their domains, such as AI’s massive and automated damage to SEO (Search Engine Optimization) in recent years. If any group is worth waging literal war on, it is insurance, marketing, and DRM companies globally, as well as other similar parasites who could only claim to be a part of “humanity” in the strictest biological sense of the word.

In contrast, you need look no further than the current state of the music industry to see what more effective methods look like. The piracy of music has massively decreased since the days of Napster, with even the few remaining by 2017 reduced by a further 65% by 2021. The simple reality is that the inconvenience of downloading and storing individual songs (often with inconsistent quality) on a local device in order to play any music is usually far greater than the monetary cost of paying for a streaming service subscription.

The remaining music piracy has come to mirror the streaming services themselves, accounting for a reported 80% of global activity. That means that the bulk of this activity which remains only manages to do so by reducing the level of inconvenience to users. https://blog.dataprot.net/piracy-statistics

This clearly illustrates how those with disposable income (as in the people with actual money, not imaginary money) will gladly pay for the increased convenience of goods and services. This same basic principle is also clearly observed with food delivery (vastly superior in Southeast Asia), taxis, Amazon’s shopping (in the US), and numerous other industries, where convenience is a key competitive value.

The music streaming sites largely offer the same spread of content, unlike the dumpster fires calling themselves “streaming video platforms”, marking a sharp point of deviation, where the majority of competitive value is lost in the case of video. Even though both music and video streaming services have the same laughably trivial to break DRM “protections”, the music streaming services tend to offer vastly superior content and convenience compared to their video counterparts.

This illustrates that one of the best ways to actually reduce piracy is simply to treat it like a competitor, making sure that you offer greater convenience, as well as more and higher quality content. For those without the money to pay for services, piracy will remain, as they can’t exchange money for convenience and selection, but they were never going to be paying customers anyway.

If a company and their services can’t offer more and higher quality content and convenience than piracy, they clearly shouldn’t exist.

Piracy also often serves as an interim step preceding the purchase of software. This is commonly seen in the gaming industry, but also among other useful software tools, where a pirated copy of something is treated like a demo of the software. The vast majority of games have features that are wholly impractical and often buggy to engage with, such as co-op play, for those using a pirated copy. This functionally makes those pirated copies a demo for a given player to gauge if the game is worth paying for.

Given the current state of the gaming industry (not to be confused with the gambling industry, which sometimes calls itself “gaming”), there is a greater need for such demos than ever. Steam itself is also another example of a predatory company that scams developers out of 30% of their revenue (in other words, 30% price hikes to consumers), similar to the app stores of Google and Apple.
