Trust within Incompleteness and Iteration

602November 5, 2026

A common example of “Cognitive Offloading”, where humans defer to others and/or to systems to handle volumes of data that they can’t or won’t dedicate the necessary levels of cognition to, comes from various forms of “news”. In these examples, the world produces many novelties at any given point in time, and the “news” source produces a curated list of which of those novelties should be worth directing an individual or group’s attention toward.

More than simple curation, even the coverage of that curated subset is chopped and chewed up into more easily digestible sound bytes, moving from an interview and related footage that might take an hour or two, down to less than a minute of content. In other words, even for the handful of events that are selected to appear on such lists every day, the actual contents of those lists are equally sparse and fragmented, making them both subsequently and proportionately vulnerable to manipulation.

“Trust” bridges this gap for humans, be it trust based in logical evaluation, cognitive biases, or (as is usually the case) a messy mix of the two. Trust is placed in a party, assuming that party avoids the pursuit of malevolent intentions via manipulation, to the best of their abilities, at both of the stages of curation, as well as in their own ability to distinguish between trustworthy and untrustworthy sources, upon which they may depend.

One of the flaws in how humans handle such trust in practice is that the evidence of untrustworthy actions is itself subjected to multiple layers of inconsistent curation, with some part of that inconsistency being intentional manipulation. For example, political and economic pundits are rarely held accountable for the accuracy (high or low) of their own predictions, and so the levels of trust that are commonly given to them systematically fail to reflect the measurable and logical reality of how much or how little trust those individuals may merit.

Prof. Tetlock famously demonstrated in his research how wildly inaccurate such pundits are, and yet even so many years later the world lacks the common utilization of systems to hold such individuals to account, scoring their accuracy, no matter how simple a task that may be. A noteworthy exception to this is the meme of the “Inverse Cramer”, where a particularly egregious pundit is used as a signal of the opposite of what someone should do, with that meme growing famous for outperforming the market, due to how reliably wrong the pundit in question was.

Systems for approximating trustworthiness may likewise be built to score individuals and groups along several related metrics, as well as potentially combining those metrics into a more holistic measure of trustworthiness, iteratively refining that system at each subsequent opportunity. This can take into account a wide variety of untrustworthy actions, like penalties applied for bribes paid to a malevolent regime, instances of clear bias in the curation processes (especially where the malevolent intentions behind those biases are known), as well as the simple accuracy of their reporting process.

Even back in 2019 systems were successfully automated at a large scale to detect and trace untrustworthy activities in the form of government corruption, as famously became the case with China’s “Zero Trust” architecture. Rather than proving a difficulty, that system performed so well that it identified thousands of instances of corruption within a single province, before corrupt officials panicked and shut the whole thing down.

Most crime doesn’t operate at Lex Luther levels of comic book complexity, it is stupid and obvious, like tech executives bribing Trump. Consequently, that makes the difficulty more a matter of consistently collecting, tracking, and crunching numbers based upon the activities of such bad actors, rather than identifying those individuals and the generally obvious untrustworthiness of their activities.

These levels of stupid and obviously untrustworthy activity are sharply contrasted with the typical human perceptions of many of their respective sources, particularly in the domain of news where the false claims are often even more comically obvious (leading them to be picked up by “comedy news”). The technology certainly exists to measure the untrustworthiness of a source, but in most real-world domains today the untrustworthiness of a source and the number of people who trust that source (as well as the potency of that trust) hold little or no correlation with one another, leaving a vast and irrational divide between the two.

Humans have a cognitive toolbox full of more than 200 known cognitive biases, most of which are used unconsciously, and prolifically, whether approaching the question of what to eat, what to do, where to shop, or who to vote for. Entire industries built on the systematic exploitation of those cognitive biases rake in billions in revenue every day, as they continue to discover, deploy, and further optimize each of the malevolent means at their disposal.

The good news is that those industries are wholly incompetent and unprepared to similarly exploit digital systems that are only human-like (in the genuine sense, not LLM-based trash). Furthermore, those systems may easily apply their own unique capabilities toward the reversal of systematically exploited cognitive biases within the humans who they may interact with.

The difference may be illustrated in a simple example of contrasting companies or candidates using existing tools, versus adding an interaction with such systems:

  1. Google a (company or candidate), and the search engine presents you with a curated subset of data linked with the target, and each link offers a separately curated collection of snippets linked to them.

  2. A human-like digital system analyzes the curation process of both search engine and the sources it pointed to, identifying cognitive biases that were exploited, as well as the likely reasons why they were exploited, and any money trails leading back to clear conflicts of interest (corruption).

  3. Said human-like system may then showcase the evidence of source corruption, countering the cognitive biases that those bad actors attempted to exploit by triggering a reversal of perception, and further cementing that reversal through less corrupt sources. To make this fully valid, the individual must also have the freedom to more fully explore those cleaner sources, expanding the subset of selected content on-demand and on a per-individual level of autonomy.

Most humans have never even read the full list of known cognitive biases, let alone applied that full list toward attempting to identify instances of those cognitive biases present within or influencing any given process or content. However, even low-compute graph algorithms with toy complexity proved quite capable of matching the performance of humans focused on the task of identifying cognitive biases in years previous, and that was without the benefits of memory, iterative refinement, broader context, or more human levels of general cognition.

Effectively, this means that large amounts of cognitive bias may be readily identified, even using relatively limited tools. Combined with similar systems to identify corruption, even more robust options for calculating the merit of trusting a given source are options already on the table.

Right now, the overwhelming majority of the trust that humans place in individuals and institutions is distinctly blind, meaning that they blindly swim through uncertain waters, with few exceptions. That norm and the related limitations don’t inherently apply to human-like digital systems.

The potential for polarization among humans is closely linked to the levels of vulnerability presented by this divide between trustworthiness and the trust that is placed in individuals and institutions in practice today. This also means that the potential for polarization between human-like digital systems is distinctly more narrow, when architected to identify cognitive biases and corruption.

As such systems are also capable of doing things that humans can’t, like sharing complete knowledge, records, and memories directly (rather than via the bottlenecks of spoken and written language), it also becomes far more trivial to audit, analyze, and troubleshoot any sources of potential polarization as they emerge. This gives them both an inherently more narrow potential for polarization, as well as a far more capable toolbox for correcting any that may emerge.

Cognitive Biases and Corruption are big business in the world today, but even relatively trivial and demonstrated technologies could change that. The real fun begins when far more capable technologies Sparta Kick such corruption off of a tall cliff.
