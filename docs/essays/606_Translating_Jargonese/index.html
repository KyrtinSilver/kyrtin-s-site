Translating Jargonese

606November 9, 2026

The world is full of barriers to discussion, language and cultural barriers being common and well-understood examples. However, cognitive biases encourage another equally potent set of barriers to often pass unnoticed (or intentionally overlooked), those of “Jargon”.

The real-world impact of Jargon on discussions is frequently understated, often dramatically so, due to some key distinctions separating how it functions relative to language barriers. Jargon bakes in the assumption that the other party speaks the same language (even when it is nonsense) while also implicitly threatening perceptions of incompetence should an individual prove otherwise.

This encourages the mentality of “Even a fool is thought wise if he keeps silent, and discerning if he holds his tongue.”, with variations of this quote ranging in attribution from Abraham Lincoln and Mark Twain to versions of the Bible. This mentality in turn allows for any absurdity masked in sufficient Jargon to pass through the approval processes of Boards and Committees with some regularity.

In cases where Jargon covers critical matters that justify the risks of social mis-perceptions as well as the additional cognitive costs, there are still significant risks from the potential misinterpretation of underlying meaning. These risks in turn become more acute when crossing specializations, where the same term may be used to mean something entirely different from one to the next.

The problem space of overcoming Jargon is also one of the use cases where technology actually holds some limited measure of viability, even in the laughably weak case of LLM-based technology. This possibility becomes accessible whenever and wherever a specific specialized domain or role holds some popular set of internally-understood Jargon, with the target destination for that Jargon’s translation being equally well-defined.

One of the target destinations for the translation of Jargon that is often most-needed today actually comes from the use of AI. When AI is used to imitate the work of a domain or role with abundant dependencies on Jargon, then it becomes infeasible for anyone outside of that domain or role to accurately asses the quality of any outputs.

This problem is further exacerbated by people engaging in common cognitive biases like substituting the answer to the question of “Is this output accurate?” with the answer to the question of “How do I (emotionally) feel about this output?”. The deeper and more abundant the Jargon, the more these cognitive biases are encouraged to replace actual higher cognition.

The solution is relatively simple at a conceptual level, even if it faces strict technical limitations as to where it can and cannot be applied. Software, from laughably primitive LLM-based technology to full-blown (genuine) cognitive architectures, can be created to translate any Jargon with actual and consistent meaning (within some specialty) both to other Jargon-heavy domains and roles, as well as to more commonly understood language.

Due to how LLM-based tools operate, this is also a practical necessity in many cases, since an LLM imitating a mathematician is going to function with higher fidelity when it uses the Jargon of a mathematician. It has to remain firmly within the Jargon of a domain’s training data in order to auto-complete that same data with more statistically useful levels. The same is true of Cybersecurity, or any other domains where such Jargon terms hold actual definitions.

Likewise, attempting to extract such translations of Jargon from the same tools that imitated the sources of that Jargon would be a mistake, as it shifts the window of context into a muddled cross-domain probability set. Much like LLMs infamously began “hallucinating” (the technical term is “Bullshitting”) impossible moves in chess at a high frequency following the introduction of RLHF (“Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback”) biasing them, when a secondary influence begins biasing the target, then the net result will no longer reflect the original probability distribution.

In other words, if it imitates a mathematician, and you ask it to translate mathematical Jargon into plain language, then the distribution of what it generates will no longer imitate the comprehension of a mathematician. Depending on the architecture of such tools, it may anchor more or less to the original statement, but that anchoring effect offers no guarantees (rather imitating the cognitive bias “Anchoring Effect” frequently found within training data), and it is no substitute for un-muddled comprehension.

Just as people might call upon Google Translate or similar tools to cope with language barriers today, similar tools are necessary to overcome Jargon Barriers tomorrow. Overcoming those barriers in turn also brings another substantial benefit to the table, as it allows truly meaningless Jargon to be disentangled from terms that hold some actual meaning within a domain or role.

Ironically, this applies to tasks like distinguishing the people abusing terms like “AGI” and “cognitive architecture” from those using the same terms properly, applying consistently defined and coherent meaning rather than PR-narrative drivel or the “magical thinking” of “vibe coders”. So long as people have difficulty differentiating charlatans (like OpenAI and Anthropic) from actual researchers, they remain prone to the manipulations of such bad actors.

In other words, by introducing the ability to translate any Jargon with meaning, all Jargon without meaning becomes a giant red flag, which may then be used to mark the burial sites of all those who voiced meaningless Jargon. RIP Jar-Jar-Gon, LinkedIn couldn’t have existed without you.

For those too deep in Jargon to see the surface, it is Rockwell Retro Encabulators automated all of the way down.
