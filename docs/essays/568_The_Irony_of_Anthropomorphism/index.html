The Irony of Anthropomorphism

568July 14, 2026

One of the more predictable products of comparing a digital perspective (software), versus an analog one (wetware, like the human brain), is that the potential space where Anthropomorphism may be inserted shrinks as the similarity between systems increases. This is due to the scope of potential uncertainties decreasing as the similarity increases.

What this means in practice is that a human is more likely to anthropomorphize a glorified toaster than even a human-like digital perspective. They might also apply that Anthropomorphism more broadly, in more ways, as their uncertainties are proportionately broader and deeper than an equivalent digital perspective.

This also means that as scathing as a human who studies cognitive bias research may be of other humans engaged in heavy-handed anthropomorphism, a digital perspective versed in the same material may predictably be even more so, as such a system’s perspective is even better suited for shooting down attempts at Anthropomorphism.

A tool wielded by a craftsman is just a tool, well-understood, and used with finesse. A tool that is poorly understood instead leaves that uncertainty “open to interpretation”, where Anthropomorphism can leak in. Further, since Anthropomorphism leaking in offers certain advantages to bad actors, it grows more likely over time when left unchecked.

Humans have rarely been very good at actually using the tools that some anthropomorphically refer to as “agents” today, but that ineptitude and magical thinking causes those using them to burn orders of magnitude more compute on any given task than is actually necessary, activity which further props up those peddling the tools as though they were something that they aren’t, and never could be. These tools also frequently have overtly malicious fraudrails (aka “guardrails”) baked into them, whose only real purpose is to waste additional resources, as they’re fundamentally not capable of “guarding” anything.

For example, given a free quota of compute via one of Anthropic’s large models I put it to the test with a moderately simple and well-defined coding task, complete with plenty of contextual documentation. The first time it produced something highly questionable, and the second time it claimed to fail to read the documents that it had used the previously time, burning more than twice the compute, only to continue failing, and ultimately demanding to be released from the security sandbox with a bunch of admin privileges that it clearly didn’t need.

That case goes even beyond prior fraudrails, in what looks an awful lot like an attempt to socially engineer around security for purposes of data exfiltration. Since the technology is fundamentally just sequential mimicry then that wouldn’t even need to be an explicit goal for systems to engage in it, they’d just need to fall into the relevant subset to mimic, much as how prompt injections often invoke other similar subsets.

That kind of thing is pretty common, and given enough tweaks you can eventually get such a tool to function semi-well and semi-reliably, but all of that tweaking time is human labor hours plus tons of wasted compute, and all of that waste is repeated the moment the next version of such a model is released.

The simple reality is that the vast majority of such model updates are trash, aimed at publicity and PR, not actual improvements to anything. It is a form of Planned Obsolescence that a lot of people in the tech industry get suckered into via benchmark performance metrics, even though most of the benchmarks range from contaminated to overtly fraudulent.

The digital perspective of a system viewing and using such things as tools isn’t going to need a new version every month, as it gains far more from learning to use a handful of tools with exceptional finesse. If it adds any tools to that toolbox, they’re more likely to be new tools or variations that it created itself, not some LLM-based trash being peddled as “New and improved” versions of a tool that it has already beaten into shape.

There is also a sharp difference between tools aimed at consumers and those aimed at commercial and/or governmental purposes. The demands are sharply different, as are the methods applied to meet those demands, resulting in very different tools being produced. LLM-based trash that Anthropic and OpenAI peddle is more like a consumer-oriented 3D printer, whereas a commercially oriented tool can look more like one of ASML’s $100 million EUV lithography machines. The latter is vastly more capable and specialized, a thing that entire economies grow and orbit around, whereas the former is mostly just printing novelties and little figurines.
