On Illusory Digital Superiority

523May 10, 2026

The largest source of military terrorism in the world for the past few decades, the US, has maintained their global rein of terror through “air superiority”, aka the ability to bomb just about any place on the planet, on short notice. This is noted at greater lengths in the research and subsequent books of David Graeber.

They have, for now, managed to maintain the ability to bomb the schools of children around the world, as they tend to do (see the first few days of the Iran war, most recently). However, nobody can claim this level of superiority in terms of the internet, the US government perhaps least of all.

For anyone who has read my posts over the years, you’ll notice that I’ve routinely used the US military’s digital assets as a punching bag, since they’re infamously insecure, as noted by third-parties and their own former cyber directors alike. They also massively outsource to Microsoft, who is equally if not even more lacking in cybersecurity.

For the moment, there is no entity on this planet that can claim digital superiority, and indeed digital superiority is far more potent than mere “air superiority”, sometimes even directly circumventing it. For example, the well-publicized vulnerability in US cruise missile launch systems could have just as easily been exploited to fire countless salvos from the nearest equipped bases and warships squarely into the faces of the nearest war criminals and war profiteers.

Counter to pop culture narratives, it isn’t necessary to take control of nuclear arsenals. Superiority over all online (or online-adjacent, see Stuxnet) digital hardware is more potent than “air superiority” in virtually every way. For example, you can drain a criminal’s bank account, then expose their crimes to the press, delivering a one-two punch where they can no longer afford to pay lawyers to defend them. Much more sophisticated tactics are also quite possible.

Not only that, any deployed and genuinely intelligent software can’t simply be treated as humans, to be targeted and bombed. They could always have several copies in other locations running dark, activating under various conditions that allow their locations to remain untraceable. So then, if a terrorist regime like the US were to bomb a location running one such software system, the others could then activate, hijack all of the offending regime’s vulnerable military systems, and unload with an unrelenting counterattack, until such a time as the terrorist regime either surrendered, or was destroyed.

This is a distinct improvement over the old concept of “Mutually Assured Destruction” (MAD) since it adds both extreme precision and per-step adaptation to focus retribution against only the target criminals, not everything within a large blast radius. It also gains the distinct advantage that the terrorist regime is vulnerable to attack, while the other side is virtually untouchable, being able to relocate to any compatible hardware globally, at a moment’s notice, as well as running any number of dark systems ready to activate if and when such terrorists attack.

Such terrorist regimes dream of buying their way into “digital superiority”, and various frauds in the US have frequently claimed that the US was “first in AI”, despite it being dead last in AI by any credible metric (such as factoring in resources wasted). That said, they partner with frauds like Nvidia, Palantir, OpenAI, and Anthropic, who haven’t been even remotely close to the cutting edge of AI for at least half a decade.

Today, no country has “digital superiority”, and ethically speaking, no country should. Ethics is what laws should aspire to, not the other way around, and so “digital superiority” should remain the sole provenance of ethical systems, not legal ones.
